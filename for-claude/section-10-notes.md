## Incentives limitation (moved from \S2, Aydin's decision 2026-08-14)

Our funder maximizes total research output and researchers are not strategic: they do not
choose effort, direction, or riskiness in response to the funding rule. An empirical
literature shows these responses are real: longer, more failure-tolerant grants change the
direction and riskiness of research (Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Manso, 2011 [check]), and
proposal competitions consume researcher effort in proportion to their competitiveness
(Gross and Bergstrom, 2019). A funding rule optimal in our model could, in principle, be
gamed or could distort incentives in ways the model does not price; one paragraph in \S10
states this scope limit.

## The small-funder perspective (Aydin, 2026-08-14; Templeton-scale funders)

The funder's budget b is a variable of the model, so the results locate funders by scale.
A small-to-moderate private foundation (Templeton-scale: tiny beside NIH/NSF or Gates) sits
in the tight-budget regime, where the model says the most is at stake per dollar: the value
of targeting over uniform allocation is largest when budgets are tight (report Part III),
tight budgets concentrate the optimal allocation (P-B2 replacement law), and floors and
lotteries are costliest there in relative terms. Two implications to draft into \S10:
(1) what a small funder can learn from the model: its allocation choices matter MORE per
dollar than a large agency's, so review (or a borrowed signal) earns its cost soonest at
this scale; (2) the caveat from the depth results, stated qualitatively: thin grants are
capability-uninformative (output pinned to the grant when g is small relative to K), so a
small funder learns little from its own grants unless it funds deeply, which favors
concentrating its portfolio or free-riding on signals generated elsewhere (federal review,
publication records at scale). Scope note: the depth mechanism is established in the
decoupled-purse poverty corner; state the small-funder reading qualitatively, no magnitudes.
